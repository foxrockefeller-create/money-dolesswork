Five films · One argument

Who actually makes the money?

A documentary, a satire, a 1996 polemic, a Greek finance minister and a sitting US Treasury Secretary all answer the same question — and they do not agree. Fifteen hot points on where dollars come from, who holds the pen, and what breaks next.

15 points 3 acts 6 sources Updated October 2026

Read this as a map, not a manifesto. These sources contradict each other on purpose — one says the debt is your savings, another says the money power was stolen, a third says the bill is about to arrive. Every point below is labelled with who is making the claim.

Act I

The mechanics: where a dollar actually comes from

Points 1–6. The plumbing almost nobody is taught — and the joke that lands because the plumbing really is that strange.

01

Spending comes first. Taxes come second.

A government that issues its own currency does not collect dollars and then spend them. It spends dollars into existence, then taxes some of them back out. Finding the Money follows economist Stephanie Kelton through that inversion: federal payments are keystrokes crediting bank accounts, not withdrawals from a vault somebody had to fill first.

Finding the Money · MMT

02

The debt clock is a savings clock.

Every dollar the federal government has spent and not taxed back is still out here — as cash, as bank reserves, as Treasury bonds in someone's portfolio. Kelton's reframe is that the number on the debt clock is a historical record of dollars issued and not yet retired, which is to say: the rest of us are holding it as wealth.

Finding the Money

03

Their red ink is your black ink.

This part is an accounting identity, not an opinion. The government's deficit equals the surplus of everyone else — households, firms, foreigners — to the penny. Wynne Godley's sectoral balances are the hinge of the whole film: you cannot shrink the federal deficit without shrinking private savings or widening the trade deficit. Something has to give, and the arithmetic chooses, not the politics.

Finding the Money · Wynne Godley

04

Bonds aren't a begging bowl.

In the MMT reading, Treasury issuance is a monetary operation — draining reserves, setting an interest rate, handing savers a risk-free asset — not a sovereign passing a hat because it ran out of money. A currency issuer can, in principle, set the price of its own debt. Whether it should is a different and much harder argument.

MMT · Finding the Money

05

The limit is real. It just isn't money.

You cannot run out of your own currency. You can absolutely run out of workers, housing, energy, chips and shipping capacity. MMT relocates the constraint from the budget to the real economy: inflation is the ceiling. Critics answer that this is precisely where the theory is thinnest — it names the constraint without a convincing plan for enforcing it.

MMT · and its critics

06

Nobody can explain QE with a straight face.

In October 2011, John Clarke and Bryan Dawe put "economist Avery Largenumber" on air to explain quantitative easing in under three minutes. The sketch closes the loop and then just lets it sit there: the government issues debt, the central bank creates money to buy that debt, and the money came from — the government. Two minutes of deadpan does more damage than most op-eds, because the circularity isn't the joke. The circularity is the mechanism.

Clarke and Dawe, 2011

Act II

The power: who is actually holding the pen

Points 7–12. If money is created rather than found, then creating it is a form of authority — and authority is the thing worth fighting over.

07

Most money is made by banks, not by the state.

The overwhelming bulk of the money supply is commercial bank credit: when a bank writes a loan it creates the deposit on the spot, out of nothing but a balance sheet entry. The Money Masters hammered this in 1996 and was called a crank for it. The Bank of England spelled out the same mechanics in its own 2014 quarterly bulletin.

Money Masters · Bank of England

08

The charge: the money power was privatised.

Patrick Carmack and Bill Still's three-and-a-half-hour history runs from antiquity to the 1910 meeting at Jekyll Island and the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, arguing the profit from issuing money was captured by a handful of private hands and should be returned to the public. It is a contested and often conspiratorial telling. Treat it as an argument to examine, not a verdict to accept.

Money Masters, 1996

09

Debt-based money needs more debt.

If money arrives in the world as interest-bearing credit, then servicing yesterday's loans requires issuing more tomorrow. That treadmill — not any single villain — is the film's structural grievance, and it is why it ends with a proposed Monetary Reform Act rather than a policy tweak. Note how far this is from Act I: both camps agree money is created from nothing. They draw opposite conclusions about whether that is the problem or the solution.

Money Masters, 1996

10

Central banks are insulated from you on purpose.

Independence is the design, not the bug. Defenders say it is the only thing stopping politicians from inflating the currency every election year. Yanis Varoufakis takes exactly the same fact and draws the opposite conclusion: the single most consequential economic lever has been deliberately placed beyond the reach of any vote.

Varoufakis

11

Elections that cannot reach the budget.

Varoufakis's claim comes out of 2015, when a Greek government elected on an explicit anti-austerity mandate discovered that creditors, not voters, set the budget. His argument is not that elections are rigged. It is that they do not reach the decisions that matter — and he recounts the rooms, from a midnight hotel bar with Larry Summers to the clashes with Lagarde and Schäuble, where those decisions were actually made.

Varoufakis · The Guardian

12

The ballot stops at the office door.

Capital's sphere — the workplace, the balance sheet, the bond market — is where distribution is genuinely decided, and none of it is ever put to a vote. That is the basis of Varoufakis's strongest claim: capitalism and democracy are not merely in tension, they are incompatible, and what we call liberal democracy is the performance that papers over the gap.

Varoufakis · The Guardian

“There's no such thing as democracy in our liberal democracies. What there is is a performance.”
Yanis Varoufakis — Radical Thinking with Aditya Chakrabortty, The Guardian

Act III

The reckoning: the argument as it stands in 2026

Points 13–15. What the man currently running the US Treasury says out loud, and what the crisis camp says is coming.

13

The Treasury Secretary says he cannot control the bond market.

On The Axios Show in October 2026, Scott Bessent — who had earlier declared “I am the house now” — stood by the line while draining it of swagger: “The house doesn't win every hand. The house plays percentages.” Then the admission underneath it: “I can't control the bond market. What I can do is try to get people to slow down and think.” Read Act I again and the sentence gets heavier. If the sovereign issues the currency, why is the bond market the thing with the leverage?

Bessent · The Axios Show, Oct 2026

14

“It's been a global rise.”

Pressed on surging yields, Bessent's defence was that borrowing costs are climbing everywhere, not just here: “I would be concerned if we were having some kind of idiosyncratic rise.” On prices he conceded the politics outright — “What we're not going to do is do what the Biden people did and tell the American people how good they had it.” He also argued the AI boom is not a rerun of the dot-com bubble.

Bessent · The Axios Show, Oct 2026

15

The crisis case rests on interest expense, not insolvency.

The hard-money warning — Patrick Bet-David's among them — is that debt service eventually crowds out everything else. MMT's reply is that a currency issuer never defaults; it inflates. Mainstream economics sides with neither: a 2019 Chicago survey found not one leading economist agreeing with MMT's core claims, and Krugman, Summers and Rogoff have all called it dangerous. So the honest question was never “can we pay?” It is who absorbs the adjustment — and that is a political question wearing an accountant's coat.

Bet-David · MMT critics

The disagreement

Three answers to the same five questions

All three camps accept that money is created from nothing. That is where the agreement ends.

Modern Monetary Theory

Finding the Money · Kelton · Mosler

Where money comes from
Government spending creates it; taxes destroy it.
The real limit
Inflation and real resources — never dollars.
Who's in charge
Congress, if it stopped pretending it is broke.
What breaks
Nothing financial. Manufactured austerity breaks people.
The fix
Budget for real capacity. A job guarantee as price anchor.

Monetary reform

Money Masters · Carmack & Still

Where money comes from
Private banks, as interest-bearing loans.
The real limit
None — and that is the danger, not the opportunity.
Who's in charge
Whoever holds the issuing franchise. Not the public.
What breaks
A debt treadmill that must keep growing to survive.
The fix
Return money creation to the public. End fractional reserve.

Hard money & crisis

Bet-David · the bond market

Where money comes from
Creation, yes — and it is debasement.
The real limit
Creditor confidence. It goes all at once.
Who's in charge
Bondholders. Ask the Treasury Secretary.
What breaks
Interest expense crowds out the budget, then the currency.
The fix
Spend less, own hard assets, expect the adjustment.
